What P/B and P/E tell us about PGR

Is price-to-book or price-to-earnings the better way to judge PGR's valuation? We tested both against 22 years of monthly data, then looked at what each is good for.

265 months · Sep 2004 to Sep 2026Monthly SEC filings + pricesReturns include reinvested dividends

Bottom line

  1. Neither multiple works as a “cheap means buy” signal for PGR. Low P/B did not predict better returns, and low P/E predicted worse ones.
  2. P/E's signal comes from the profitability cycle, not the price. A low P/E has usually meant return on equity (ROE) running well above normal, just before margins come back down. ROE compared with its own 5-year average is the stronger and more consistent indicator.
  3. P/B is the better tool for understanding the business. P/B was about 3.5× in both 2004 and 2026. All of PGR's return over that span came from growing book value per share and paying dividends. Today's price assumes PGR can earn about 21% on equity over the long run.

Real-time tests told the same story. Refit every year on past data only, the ROE gap beat a simple historical-average forecast by 11% of the variation in PGR's 12-month returns and by 7% against the market. P/E beat it by 4% and 0%. P/B did worse than the average (-24%).

Finding 2

The profitability cycle is what matters

P/E = P/B ÷ ROE. When PGR's P/E looked low, it was usually because ROE was unusually high, not because the price was low. Strip out the cycle by computing P/E on normal (5-year average) earnings, and the signal disappears. Measure the cycle directly, and the signal gets stronger:

How to use each measure

  • P/B: what does the price assume? Convert it to the long-run ROE it requires and ask whether the business can sustain that.
  • ROE against its own average: where are we in the cycle? Well above average has been a peak-margin warning. The combined ratio and policy growth in the monthly filings lead it.
  • P/E: read it only alongside ROE. A low P/E on peak earnings isn't a bargain; a high P/E on depressed earnings has preceded PGR's best years.
  • Book value per share growth, plus dividends: the scorecard. It explains most of what shareholders earned.

Method and caveats

  • Monthly data from PGR's SEC filings (book value, EPS, ROE), used only once each filing was public. Figures are restated across the 2006 4-for-1 split, and returns include reinvested dividends. The market is the total US stock market (VTI).
  • Correlations use bootstrap intervals and Newey-West errors for overlapping returns. Real-time tests refit each year with a gap equal to the forecast horizon. Results hold when sampling just once a year.
  • Shorter earnings windows (3 or 6 months) and shorter horizons (1 or 3 months) don't help. PGR's earnings are seasonal, and valuation works slowly.
  • 22 years is roughly 20 independent 12-month periods, so every estimate has wide uncertainty. Treat these as consistent tendencies, not forecasts.
  • Two missing months of EPS were rebuilt from quarterly filings, and five missing months of book value were interpolated.
